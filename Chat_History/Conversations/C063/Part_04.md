# A framework for almost every case question

Use this six-step sequence whenever they give you an ambiguous portfolio problem:

## 1. Define

Clarify the objective and constraints.

- Growth, loss reduction, profitability, customer access, or capital?
- Which product, channel, geography, and time horizon?
- What risk appetite or regulatory guardrails apply?

## 2. Diagnose

Confirm that the signal is real.

- Data integrity
- Population and denominator
- Policy or model changes
- Mix changes
- Seasoning effects
- Reporting changes

## 3. Decompose

Identify where the movement originates.

- Vintage
- Months on book
- Product
- Channel
- Score band
- DTI/PTI/LTV
- Term
- Exposure
- Pricing tier
- Geography
- Dealer, partner, or acquisition source

## 4. Design

Develop alternatives.

- Tighten or expand policy
- Reprice
- Change exposure
- Add counteroffers
- Create manual-review treatment
- Introduce segmentation
- Change monitoring thresholds

## 5. Decide

Compare risk-adjusted economics and controls.

- Incremental approval or volume
- Incremental Expected Loss
- Margin and capital effects
- Customer and compliance implications
- Operational capacity
- Implementation risk

## 6. Detect

Define post-launch monitoring.

- Leading indicators
- Vintage performance
- Trigger thresholds
- Champion/challenger results
- Exceptions
- Escalation and rollback criteria

This will make your answers sound like portfolio leadership rather than isolated analytics.

## 6. “What is Expected Loss?”

```text
Expected Loss = PD × LGD × EAD
```

Explain each component:

- **PD:** likelihood of default over the applicable horizon
- **LGD:** severity conditional on default
- **EAD:** exposure when default occurs

## 7. “What is the difference between a model and a credit strategy?”

> A model estimates or ranks risk. A strategy determines what action to take with that information.
>
> The model may produce a score or PD. The strategy combines that output with policy rules, product limits, affordability, exposure, pricing, compliance, and operational constraints to assign an outcome such as approve, decline, review, counteroffer, or line adjustment.
>
> A good model does not automatically create a good strategy. Strategy performance depends on cutoffs, overlays, treatment paths, exceptions, implementation, and monitoring.

## 11. “How do you distinguish discrimination from calibration?”

> **Discrimination** measures whether the model ranks higher-risk observations above lower-risk observations. AUC, Gini, KS, and concordance are examples.
>
> **Calibration** measures whether predicted probabilities align with observed outcomes.
>
> A model can rank accounts well but systematically over- or underpredict risk. For portfolio decisions, both matter: discrimination supports segmentation, while calibration influences pricing, Expected Loss, capital, and policy thresholds.

Mention your Survival Strategy Framework:

## 13. “How did you use survival analysis in credit risk?”

> Survival analysis is useful when the timing of the event matters and some observations have not yet experienced the event.
>
> At the OCC, I used Cox proportional hazards and discrete-time survival/logistic methods to analyze delinquency, default, and prepayment by loan age. Right-censoring allowed active loans to contribute valid time-at-risk information rather than being discarded.
>
> That was especially valuable for comparing term structures and life-of-loan behavior because a five-year loan and a seven-year loan should not be evaluated as if they have identical risk timing.
>
> My current Python framework extends that discipline into reusable cross-validation, calibration, scenario, and stakeholder-evidence architecture.

Be prepared to define a hazard ratio:

> A hazard ratio above one indicates higher instantaneous event risk relative to the reference, holding other modeled factors constant; below one indicates lower hazard.

## Case 2: “The business wants 10% more volume without increasing the loss rate.”

> I would not treat the portfolio as one average risk pool.
>
> I would identify segments near current policy boundaries and evaluate their marginal risk-adjusted economics. Options could include:
>
> - modest policy expansion in well-performing cells;
> - reduced exposure rather than full decline;
> - counteroffers;
> - price changes;
> - term changes;
> - manual review for information-sensitive cases;
> - partner or channel targeting.
>
> I would run the current and challenger strategies on the same population, compare approval, booked exposure, Expected Loss, margin, concentrations, and operational burden, and then design a controlled champion/challenger test.

> The best answer may not be a single relaxed cutoff. It may be a segmented combination of policy, price, and exposure.

## Case 3: “A competitor cuts rates. Should we match?”

> Not automatically.
>
> I would analyze the rate difference by segment, customer price sensitivity, take-up, prepayment, expected loss, funding cost, capital, and adverse-selection risk.
>
> Matching broadly may retain volume but destroy risk-adjusted margin. Not matching may cause the strongest applicants to leave and worsen the remaining risk mix.
>
> I would identify where elasticity and lifetime value justify a response, test targeted pricing or retention offers, and monitor both take-up and post-booking risk.
>
> The decision should be based on marginal risk-adjusted return and selection effects—not competitor price alone.

# Key technical distinctions

## Model vs. strategy

> A model estimates or ranks risk. A strategy decides what action to take.

```text
Model output
+ policy
+ affordability
+ exposure
+ price
+ compliance
+ operations
= decision
```

## Discrimination vs. calibration

- **Discrimination:** Does the model rank risk correctly?
  - AUC, Gini, KS, concordance
- **Calibration:** Do predicted probabilities align with actual outcomes?

## Vintage vs. portfolio average

- Portfolio average can hide mix changes.
- Compare vintages at the same months on book.
- Separate:
  - different mix;
  - worse performance within the same risk cells.

## PD vs. loss

High PD does not automatically mean highest dollar loss.

```text
Loss depends on:
PD + exposure + severity
```

# Likely case questions

## “Losses increased. What do you do?”

> Confirm the data and seasoning, then decompose by vintage, months on book, channel, score, term, exposure, price, geography, and policy tier. Determine whether the increase is caused by mix, worse within-cell performance, recovery deterioration, or measurement changes. Recommend targeted policy, pricing, exposure, verification, or servicing actions and define monitoring and rollback thresholds.

## “We need 10% more volume without increasing loss rate.”

> Identify profitable populations near current policy boundaries. Test segmented policy expansion, reduced exposure, counteroffers, pricing, term changes, or manual review. Compare current and challenger strategies on the same population and evaluate incremental approval, exposure, Expected Loss, margin, concentration, and operational burden.

## “A competitor cuts price. Should we match?”

> Evaluate elasticity, take-up, adverse selection, prepayment, funding cost, Expected Loss, and capital. A broad match may destroy margin; no response may drive away stronger applicants. Use targeted pricing where risk-adjusted return supports it.

## “How do you set risk appetite?”

> Define acceptable loss, return, concentration, access, capital, compliance, and operational outcomes. Translate them into measurable limits, early-warning triggers, escalation rules, and rollback criteria by product and segment.

## “How do you validate a policy change?”

```text
Define rule
→ identify affected population
→ run current and challenger on same population
→ compare risk, approval, exposure, margin, and customer outcomes
→ stress test
→ pilot
→ monitor
```

# Behavioral-Interview Rules

Wells Fargo’s guidance uses **SBO**:

- **Situation / Task:** Establish one specific past event, business problem, decision, population, and stakes.
- **Behavior:** Explain what **I personally did**, why I did it, how I analyzed the problem, who I influenced, and how I handled obstacles.
- **Outcome:** State the decision, quantified result, control improvement, learning, or final resolution.

Follow these rules throughout our preparation:

- Use past-oriented, specific examples.
- Use “I” and “me” for my actions—not vague “we” statements.
- Acknowledge the team only to establish context.
- Keep the Behavior section as the largest part of the answer.
- End with a concrete outcome.
- Use different examples across questions when possible.
- Prepare successful, difficult, and unsuccessful examples.
- Avoid theoretical “I would” answers when a past example exists.
- Avoid “generally,” “usually,” and generic leadership claims.
- Do not fabricate a metric merely to make a story sound stronger.
- Be prepared for follow-up probes on any part of the example.
- It is acceptable to pause, take notes, or ask for clarification.
- If I lack an exact past example, state the boundary briefly, use the closest relevant example, and explain the transfer without overstating direct ownership.

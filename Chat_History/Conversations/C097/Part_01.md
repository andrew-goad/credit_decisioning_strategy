# Behavioral-interview rules

Wells Fargo’s guidance uses **SBO**:

- **Situation / Task:** Establish one specific past event, business problem, decision, population, and stakes.
- **Behavior:** Explain what **I personally did**, why I did it, how I analyzed the problem, who I influenced, and how I handled obstacles.
- **Outcome:** State the decision, quantified result, control improvement, learning, or final resolution.

Follow these rules throughout our preparation:

- Use past-oriented, specific examples.
- Use “I” and “me” for my actions—not vague “we” statements.
- Acknowledge the team only to establish context.
- Keep the Behavior section as the largest part of the answer.
- End with a concrete outcome.
- Use different examples across questions when possible.
- Prepare successful, difficult, and unsuccessful examples.
- Avoid theoretical “I would” answers when a past example exists.
- Avoid “generally,” “usually,” and generic leadership claims.
- Do not fabricate a metric merely to make a story sound stronger.
- Be prepared for follow-up probes on any part of the example.
- It is acceptable to pause, take notes, or ask for clarification.
- If I lack an exact past example, state the boundary briefly, use the closest relevant example, and explain the transfer without overstating direct ownership.

For each major story, develop:

1. A **20–30 second headline**.
2. A **60–90 second interview answer**.
3. A **2–3 minute expanded answer** for technical probing.
4. Likely follow-up questions.
5. Facts or claims that must not be overstated.

Important pricing work included:

- deriving APR from origination amount, monthly payment, and loan term;
- validating APR stability over time;
- comparing aggregate APR results with Experian AutoCount;
- defining spread as APR minus a same-maturity Treasury rate from the origination month;
- constructing missing four-year and six-year Treasury benchmarks from adjacent maturities;
- comparing spreads across term, origination year, credit-score segment, and lender type;
- using OLS pricing analysis;
- evaluating whether higher residual delinquency risk was reflected in price.

The research found, after controlling for observed risk factors and macroeconomic conditions, that six- and seven-plus-year Auto loans generally carried higher residual 90-DPD risk than shorter loans throughout their lives. At the same time, spreads on longer-term loans compressed relative to five-year and shorter terms, supporting evidence of an increasingly inverted Auto-loan yield curve and suggesting that incremental term risk was not fully reflected in customer pricing.

# 8. Auto Pricing and credit-loss-assumption framework

## A. What a pricing loss assumption actually is

A pricing loss assumption is not merely a model probability. It is a **governed, versioned estimate of the credit cost expected from a defined origination population**, used within a financial return framework.

The complete assumption package ordinarily contains:

```text
Defined population and effective date
+ outcome and loss definitions
+ segmentation
+ default / delinquency or loss model
+ calibration
+ macroeconomic treatment
+ exposure and amortization path
+ prepayment assumptions
+ recovery / LGD assumptions
+ overlays or management adjustments
+ implementation mapping
+ limitations and monitoring thresholds
```

The Senior Lead may not personally own every component. Finance may govern the return metric, Model Risk may perform independent validation, Pricing may own the final price architecture, and Technology may own deployment. But this role must understand how the components fit together and be able to challenge the full chain.

The operating flow is:

```text
Application, bureau, vehicle, dealer, channel, and contract data
→ underwriting and risk segmentation
→ monthly default, exposure, prepayment, and recovery paths
→ lifetime expected credit loss
→ funding, acquisition, servicing, capital, and revenue model
→ price / amount / term recommendation
→ booked vintage
→ actual-versus-expected monitoring
→ recalibration, overlay, segmentation change, or redevelopment
```

## B. Expected Loss: the interview-ready version

The familiar shorthand is:

```text
Expected Loss = PD × LGD × EAD
```

That is useful, but a pricing-loss role requires the time-dependent version:

```text
Lifetime EL
= Σ over time t [
    probability the loan remains active through t−1
    × conditional probability of default in t
    × exposure at default in t
    × loss severity in t
    × discount factor, where applicable
  ]
```

A compact mathematical form is:

```text
EL = Σt St−1 × hD,t × EADt × LGDt × DFt
```

Where:

- **\(S_{t-1}\)** is survival through the prior period without default, prepayment, or maturity.
- **\(h_{D,t}\)** is the conditional default hazard in period \(t\).
- **EAD\(_t\)** reflects scheduled amortization and the balance remaining when default occurs.
- **LGD\(_t\)** reflects the shortfall after repossession, auction proceeds, recoveries, costs, and timing.
- **DF\(_t\)** reflects discounting when required by the applicable pricing or financial methodology.

### Why timing matters

Two loans with the same headline lifetime default rate can have different economic loss:

- one may default early at a high balance and high LTV;
- the other may default late after substantial principal paydown;
- one may have fast recovery at a strong auction value;
- the other may have delayed recovery and high repossession costs;
- one may prepay rapidly, reducing interest income and future exposure;
- the other may remain outstanding through the full term.

The role therefore needs **loss curves and cash-flow timing**, not just one static PD.

## C. How Expected Loss enters pricing economics

A general risk-adjusted contribution framework is:

```text
Present value of interest and fee income
+ present value of other revenue
− funding or transfer-pricing cost
− acquisition / dealer / channel cost
− operating and servicing cost
− expected credit loss
− capital or risk charge
= risk-adjusted contribution or NPV
```

The exact internal metric could be contribution margin, ROA, RAROC, NPV, or another Finance-approved return measure. You should never assume Wells Fargo’s internal formula.

Three distinctions are critical.

### 1. Customer APR is not automatically the lender’s economic yield

In indirect Auto lending, the lender generally establishes a buy rate and acceptable structure, while a dealer may offer the customer a marked-up APR within applicable rules and program constraints. Dealer reserve, add-ons, fees, subvention, and the final amount financed can materially change the economics and selection of booked loans.

The OCC paper describes the relationship among lender buy rate, customer APR, dealer spread, and backend products, while also noting that customer APR in the study was inferred from scheduled payments rather than observed lender economics. fileciteturn0file5L534-L552

### 2. Treasury spread in your OCC work was a research benchmark

The paper defined spread as:

```text
Customer APR − same-maturity Treasury rate from the origination month
```

That controlled for prevailing rates and Treasury term structure. It was not the same as a bank’s net interest margin, matched funding spread, or full risk-adjusted contribution. Production pricing would ordinarily use the institution’s approved funding or transfer-pricing framework and incorporate expenses, loss, capital, and other revenue.

### 3. A higher APR does not necessarily create a better loan

A higher APR can improve contractual yield but can also:

- reduce take-up;
- change adverse selection;
- increase payment burden;
- affect prepayment behavior;
- alter dealer placement;
- shift competitive position;
- or produce fair-lending and customer-treatment concerns if rules and exceptions are not governed properly.

Price, approval, amount, term, and policy must therefore be evaluated together.

## F. Why term is especially important in Auto


## G. Monitoring actual versus expected loss

A useful executive measure is:

```text
A/E at month-on-book m
= cumulative actual net loss through MOB m
÷ cumulative expected net loss through MOB m
```

It must compare like with like:

- same seasoning;
- same population and denominator;
- same net/gross-loss definition;
- same segment;
- same vintage treatment;
- and consistent recovery timing.

A forecast miss should be decomposed in this order:

```text
1. Data, source, or definition change
2. Portfolio-mix change
3. Within-segment performance change
4. Macroeconomic change
5. Recovery or LGD change
6. Exposure, prepayment, or timing change
7. Model or calibration error
8. Implementation or mapping error
```

Do not jump immediately from “A/E is high” to “redevelop the model.”

### Choosing the appropriate corrective action

- **Recalibration:** Model structure and rank ordering remain useful, but the forecast level is persistently biased.
- **Temporary overlay:** A measurable, time-limited condition is not yet adequately represented in the model; the overlay needs rationale, approval, monitoring, and an exit condition.
- **Segmentation change:** Aggregate performance conceals systematic differences across term, score, LTV, dealer, vehicle, channel, or another economically meaningful factor.
- **Redevelopment:** Relationships, variables, functional form, population, or business environment have changed enough that the existing model is no longer conceptually or empirically adequate.

# Technical interview responses

## 1. Why logistic regression rather than survival analysis?

> “I would first clarify that logistic regression and survival analysis are not always alternatives. A standard binary logistic regression is appropriate when the business question has a fixed horizon—such as whether an account defaults within the next 12 months—and the observations have comparable follow-up periods. Survival analysis is preferable when the timing of default, seasoning, unequal observation periods, and censoring matter.
>
> “A discrete-time survival model can itself be estimated with logistic regression. You restructure the data into account-period observations and model the probability of first default in the next period, conditional on the account having survived to that point. That was the approach used in the long-term Auto study: a loan-year survival panel with first 90 DPD as the event and prepayment or maturity as exits.” fileciteturn0file5L260-L298

**Memory line:**

> **Fixed-horizon outcome → binary logistic. Time-to-event, seasoning, or censoring → survival. Discrete-time survival may still use a logistic link.**

## 2. When would you use CoxPH versus discrete-time hazard?

> “I would use Cox proportional hazards when I have reasonably precise event timing, want to estimate relative hazard without imposing a parametric baseline-hazard shape, and the proportional-hazards assumption is defensible.
>
> “I would use discrete-time hazard when performance is observed in monthly, quarterly, or annual intervals; when there are many tied event times; or when I need period-specific PD estimates that can incorporate loan age and a changing macroeconomic path. That often maps naturally to credit-loss forecasting because I can produce a Year 1, Year 2, and Year 3 conditional PD path.
>
> “In the published long-term Auto analysis, the annual loan-year structure made discrete-time logistic survival the natural choice. My broader OCC work included CoxPH, and my current Survival Retention and Scenario Engine gives me a recent hands-on CoxPH example. I would not call the published Auto model CoxPH.” fileciteturn0file5L260-L298 fileciteturn0file6L81-L87 fileciteturn0file6L122-L124

**Key trade-off:**

| CoxPH | Discrete-time hazard |
|---|---|
| Exact or continuous event timing | Monthly, quarterly, or annual observations |
| Relative hazard interpretation | Direct period-specific conditional PD |
| Unspecified baseline hazard | Explicit time effects or baseline-hazard shape |
| Requires proportional-hazards assessment | Easier to allow term, age, and period interactions |
| Can use time-varying covariates | Especially intuitive for scenario-by-period forecasting |

## 3. How would you model default and prepayment?

> “I would treat default and prepayment as competing exits because once a loan prepays, it is no longer available to default and no longer produces the originally expected interest stream.
>
> “One approach is to estimate separate cause-specific hazard models—one for default and one for prepayment—using the same active risk set. That allows the two outcomes to have different drivers. Another option is multinomial logistic regression if I want to model mutually exclusive next-period states jointly and its assumptions are acceptable.
>
> “For each period, I would combine the probability the loan is still active with its conditional default hazard. The default probability arising specifically in period \(t\) is the active survival probability entering the period multiplied by the conditional default PD in that period. For pricing, I would then combine that default-incidence path with EAD and LGD, while separately recognizing that prepayment reduces both future credit exposure and future revenue.”

In the long-term Auto study, delinquency was modeled separately from prepayment because the relevant drivers could differ; loans exited after 90 DPD, prepayment, or maturity. fileciteturn0file5L260-L268

A simple discrete competing-risk structure is:

\[
S_t=S_{t-1}(1-h^D_t-h^P_t)
\]

\[
P(\text{default specifically in }t)=S_{t-1}h^D_t
\]

where:

- \(h^D_t\) = conditional default hazard;
- \(h^P_t\) = conditional prepayment hazard;
- \(S_{t-1}\) = probability the loan remains active entering the period.

## 5. How would you create a lifetime PD curve?

> “I would begin with historical account-period data and estimate the conditional probability of default during each future period, given that the account has survived to the beginning of that period. > “If the Year 1 conditional PD is 2%, then 98% of the probability mass survives into Year 2. If the Year 2 conditional PD is 3%, then 2.94% of the original probability mass defaults specifically in Year 2. For a two-year loan, the cumulative PD is therefore:
>
> \[
> 1-(0.98\times0.97)=4.94\%
> \]
>
> “With prepayment, I would reduce the active probability mass for both default and prepayment. \[
m^D_{i,t}=S_{i,t-1}h^D_{i,t}
\]

\[
Lifetime\ PD_i=\sum_{t=1}^{T}m^D_{i,t}
\]

The pricing-loss extension is:

\[

=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
m^D_{i,t}
\times EAD_{i,t}
\times LGD_{i,t}

## 6. What assumptions does each method require?

These are general modeling assumptions and validation considerations—not claims that every one was documented identically in each of your OCC assignments.

| Method | Principal assumptions and risks |
|---|---|
| **Binary logistic regression** | Consistent fixed horizon; correct event definition; observations independent or appropriately clustered; linearity in the log-odds for continuous predictors unless transformed; representative development sample; no target leakage; acceptable calibration. |
| **Discrete-time hazard/logistic** | Correct account-period risk set; variables measured before the outcome interval; censoring reasonably non-informative after conditioning; appropriate interval length; baseline time/seasoning effects correctly specified; within-account dependence addressed. |
| **CoxPH** | Proportional hazards unless explicitly relaxed; correct time origin; non-informative censoring conditional on covariates; appropriate functional form; accurate event timing; no leakage from post-event information. |
| **Multinomial logistic** | Outcomes are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive; standard multinomial logit generally assumes independence of irrelevant alternatives; covariate specification is suitable across outcome categories; adequate observations for each outcome. |
| **OLS pricing regression** | Correct conditional-mean specification; errors uncorrelated with explanatory variables; linearity or suitable transformations; no severe multicollinearity; residual and influence diagnostics; robust errors when heteroskedasticity is present. |
| **Decision tree** | No classical distributional assumptions, but results depend heavily on representative data, adequate node size, pruning/depth controls, split stability, treatment of missing values, and out-of-time performance. |
| **Marginal-PD term structure** | Historical conditional-default relationships remain relevant; future loan-age and macro paths are defensible; competing exits are handled; period definitions are consistent; calibration remains valid across the horizon. |

> **The development data, model implementation, and monitoring data must use consistent populations, definitions, time horizons, and outcomes.**

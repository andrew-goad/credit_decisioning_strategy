A modern lending decision stack needs to do more than predict default.

It needs to connect prediction to action.

In my view, a strong decision stack needs at least six layers:

✅ risk prediction
Who is likely to default, roll forward, repay, or respond?

✅ economic objective
What outcome are we optimising for — loss, revenue, NPV, profitability, growth, or capital efficiency?

✅ policy and risk appetite
What risks are we willing to take, and where are the boundaries?

✅ offer decisioning
What should we offer — amount, tenor, price, limit, top-up, or decline?

✅ execution layer
Is the approved strategy actually what goes live in production?

✅ monitoring and feedback
Are the decisions producing the intended portfolio outcomes?

This is where many lending systems become more interesting.

A model can be strong, but if the economic objective is unclear, the decision logic is fragmented, or monitoring is weak, the business may still underperform.

The point is not to build more complexity.

The point is to make the decision chain clearer.

Because in lending, value is not created by prediction alone.

It is created when prediction, policy, economics, and execution work together.

When I applied to TestBox, I had no idea what synthetic data was.

For the technical challenge, I needed to build a data pipeline for data that did not even exist yet. I knew how to build the pipeline, but what about the data? Until then, my experience with generative AI was asking things in ChatGPT and writing simple prompts for database migrations.

As the first datasets started coming out, an even harder question emerged than how to write the prompts: how would I know if that synthetic data was actually good?

Today, I know that the best synthetic datasets mainly have 3 characteristics:

1. Correct relationships and dependencies

A row, or even an entire table, may look valid when analyzed on its own, but the dataset completely breaks when the relationships do not make sense. This goes far beyond respecting FKs in an ERD. Relationships need to follow business rules, reflect real dependencies, and help tell the story of the system. An enterprise customer would generally not have small deals and short sales cycles, since companies of that size have more complex approval processes and both technical, legal, and financial evaluations. Financial systems would hardly have extremely high transactions on newly created accounts without generating validation, risk, or compliance events. A loyal customer would probably not spend a long period without making purchases or interactions, since users with a consistent engagement history tend to maintain relatively predictable behavior patterns.

2. Natural inconsistencies

Real datasets have natural imperfections: delays, out-of-range values, temporal gaps, and inconsistent behaviors are also part of reality. In a real-world scenario, users forget to complete processes, a deal gets frozen due to lack of customer response, a shopping cart is abandoned during checkout. There is a great concern about making a synthetic dataset look perfect and, in that process, we often lose sight of what the customer actually needs: the broadest possible coverage of scenarios and use cases, whether positive or negative.

3. Human communication patterns

Systems are built by people and used by people. Because of that, the way individuals communicate is also part of the reality that a synthetic dataset needs to reproduce. The tone of a conversation, an email, or a message is consistent with the position the person holds, their area of expertise, and even the size and culture of the company. An enterprise manager is generally more formal and strategic, while the CEO of a startup tends to be more direct, energetic, and informal. This matters a lot in synthetic datasets created for product demos. A meeting recording and analysis company, for example, needs conversations that are very close to real interactions in order to demonstrate the full capabilities of the platform to prospective customers. When everyone communicates in the same way and addresses the necessary topics, the dataset may be correct, but it loses an important part of its realism.

The quality of a synthetic dataset is rarely about using the best LLM model or building the most sophisticated pipeline. The better we understand the business, its processes, and its users, the better the synthetic data we produce tends to be. In the end, quality lies in the ability to transform business rules, behaviors, and real-world processes into data that continues to make sense when someone starts analyzing it.
